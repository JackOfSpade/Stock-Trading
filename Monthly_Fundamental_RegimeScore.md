# Regime Condition Axis Scoring

## growth_momentum: stable

Q1 2026 GDP advance printed +2.0% annualized — a sequential acceleration off Q4 2025 final +0.5% — but Q4 weakness was attributed to a one-off shutdown drag, leaving the underlying run-rate close to trend rather than re-accelerating. April non-farm payrolls at +115k moderated below March's revised +185k, and the Feb-Mar combined revision was −16k, consistent with a labor market that has stabilized after a February negative print rather than re-accelerated. Reported earnings momentum is unusually strong (84% beat rate, +27.1% blended growth, record 14.7% net margin), but the strength is concentrated in margins and a few sectors and reflects the prior quarter's activity, while concurrent retail sales and GDP show pace approximately in line with — not above — the medium-term run-rate.

## inflation_trend: reaccelerating

The Q1 2026 PCE price index printed +4.5% annualized — a material upward shift — and the March CPI release showed headline +3.3% YoY with core +2.6% YoY (+0.2% MoM core), while March PPI final demand reached +4.0% YoY, the largest 12-month advance since February 2023. The proximate drivers are a sustained energy-supply shock (Brent ~$101–128/bbl during the month, well above pre-war baseline) compounded by a major April 2 tariff escalation (100% on patented pharmaceuticals and 15–50% on steel/aluminum/copper derivatives, with effective average tariff rate ~11.8%). Both supply-side channels are still in pass-through; central-bank communication explicitly states the inflation shock has not yet peaked.

## policy_stance: hawkish

The April 28–29 FOMC held the funds target at 3.50–3.75% on an 8–4 vote, but three of the four dissenters specifically opposed retaining the easing bias — a directionally hawkish dissent pattern even within a hold. The post-meeting statement added language tying elevated inflation to global energy prices and flagging Middle East developments as a source of high outlook uncertainty, and the Chair stated rates are "in a good place" with explicit signaling of willingness to hike if needed and a desire to see "the back side" of the energy shock before any cut. The reference dot plot (March 2026 SEP) had already lifted the longer-run anchor to 3.0%; April communications tighten this further at the margin.

## risk_sentiment: neutral

Cross-asset signals split: investment-grade corporate spreads are characterized as near 25-year tights (~80 bps OAS), the S&P 500 reclaimed war-related losses by mid-April, and the VIX decompressed from a 31 peak in late March to a low-twenties / upper-teens range through April (reported monthly average ~17). At the same time, the VIX remains above the early-2025 norm, the USD index is roughly flat (−0.2% MoM, range 98–101), and ceasefire fragility was openly priced (April 12 negotiation collapse, blockade threat). The combination — risk-on credit/earnings signals offset by an unresolved shock backdrop — reads as recovering toward, but not at, a clean risk-on regime; "stressed" is excluded by the credit-spread evidence.

## shock_overlay: latent

The active phase of the late-February geopolitical shock paused with the April 7–8 U.S.–Iran ceasefire (Pakistan-mediated, including Israel), but the shock's economic transmission persists through April: only ~191 vessels traversed the Strait of Hormuz the entire month versus its normal volume, Brent stayed elevated at ~$101–103/bbl, and the ceasefire's durability was openly contested (April 12 negotiation breakdown, U.S. blockade threat). A second-channel shock was added domestically via the April 2 tariff EO. "None" is excluded because real economic transmission (oil, shipping) is ongoing; "acute" is excluded because the kinetic phase paused and equity/credit markets functioned normally through the month.

April 2026 reads as stable growth + reaccelerating inflation + hawkish policy + neutral risk sentiment + latent shock overlay — a stagflation-tilted regime in active recovery from a late-February geopolitical shock, with the recovery itself contested. The dominant cross-axis tension is between strong reported corporate momentum (record earnings, tight credit) and a Fed forced into a more hawkish posture by oil-driven and tariff-driven inflation pass-through that has not yet peaked.
