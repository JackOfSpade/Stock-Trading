Section 5 — Policy environment in March 2026
Supreme Court tariff ruling (effective February, with March aftermath). In Learning Resources / V.O.S. Selections v. Trump, decided 20 February 2026, the Supreme Court held 6–3 that the President lacks authority under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) RV Industry Association to impose the "reciprocal" tariffs that had been in effect since April 2025. Following the ruling the administration used Section 122 of the 1974 Trade Act to substitute a 10% blanket tariff on most imports, valid for 150 days unless extended by Congress (UK Parliament Commons Library brief, US trade tariffs; Wikipedia, Tariffs in the second Trump administration; Reed Smith tariff tracker). The government had collected approximately $166 billion in now-invalidated IEEPA tariffs; CBP began working on a refund process during March (Wikipedia, Liberation Day tariffs).
Tariff developments in March 2026. On 27 March 2026 the U.S. Court of International Trade issued an amended order directing CBP to reliquidate all entries where liquidation was final, excluding IEEPA tariffs — an administrative step toward refunds (Reed Smith tariff tracker, March 30 update). Still-in-force tariffs during March included: Section 232 steel/aluminum (50%, set 4 June 2025); 25% on automobiles (3 April 2025) and auto parts (3 May 2025); Section 301 tariffs on China (pre-existing); and the new Section 122 10% blanket duty. A Section 232 review of steel/aluminum/copper concluded on 2 April 2026 (one day after month-end) with a presidential proclamation modifying the measures effective 6 April (Reed Smith tracker; RVIA tariff tracker).

Closing character of March 2026
March 2026 was a regime-breaking month. Entering the month with the S&P 500 Equal-Weight Index at all-time highs, investors were repriced violently by (a) a war-driven oil shock (Brent +51% MoM), (b) the worst U.S. February payroll print since late 2024 with unemployment ticking back to 4.4%, (c) firming PPI and CPI tendencies before the energy shock even hit CPI, and (d) a March FOMC that held policy at 3.50–3.75% and nudged the dot-plot distribution more hawkish. Broad equity indices fell 4.5–6%, ten of eleven S&P sectors were negative, Treasury yields jumped ~40 bp across the curve, and the Nasdaq entered correction. The dominant impulse was macro de-risking, not style rotation — the textbook stagflation-squeeze setup of higher inflation, slowing growth, and reduced Fed flexibility.
